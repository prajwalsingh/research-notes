To understand the "nitty-gritty" of Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression, we have to pick up exactly where Principal Component Regression (PCR) failed.While PCR extracts latent components ($Z$) by looking only at the variance within the features ($X$), PLS is a supervised dimensionality reduction technique. It extracts latent components by maximizing the covariance between the features ($X$) and the target variable ($Y$).In PLS, a feature will only be bundled into a principal component if it varies a lot and if that variation mathematically moves in tandem with the target you are trying to predict.Here is the step-by-step mathematical engine behind PLS, specifically focusing on the most common algorithm used to compute it: the NIPALS (Non-linear Iterative Partial Least Squares) algorithm for a single target variable (PLS1).The PLS Objective: The Covariance MaximizationIn PCR, we wanted to find a weight vector $w$ that maximizes the variance of our new component: $\text{Var}(Xw)$.In PLS, we want to find a weight vector $w$ that maximizes the covariance between our new component and the target:$$w = \arg\max_{\vert{}\vert{}w\vert{}\vert{}=1} \text{Cov}(Xw, Y)$$The Step-by-Step NIPALS AlgorithmAssume you have an $n \times p$ feature matrix $X$ and an $n \times 1$ target vector $Y$.Step 1: StandardizationJust like PCR, PLS is highly scale-dependent.You must mean-center and scale every column in $X$ so they have a mean of $0$ and a standard deviation of $1$.You must mean-center the target vector $Y$.Let us call these starting matrices $X_0$ and $Y_0$.Step 2: The Iterative Loop (Extracting Component $k$)Instead of doing a massive matrix decomposition all at once (like SVD in PCR), PLS extracts the latent components one by one in an iterative loop. For the first component ($k=1$):A. Calculate the $X$ Weights ($w_k$)We find the direction in the $X$ space that is most highly correlated with $Y$. We compute the cross-product of $X$ and $Y$:$$w_k = X_{k-1}^T Y_{k-1}$$We then normalize this weight vector so its length is $1$:$$w_k = \frac{w_k}{\vert{}\vert{}w_k\vert{}\vert{}}$$(Intuition: $w_k$ tells us how much each original feature aligns with the target $Y$. Features that move with $Y$ get high weights; features that are random noise get near-zero weights.)B. Calculate the $X$ Scores ($t_k$)We project our original data onto this new weight vector to create our first PLS component (the score vector):$$t_k = X_{k-1} w_k$$(Intuition: $t_k$ is an $n \times 1$ column vector. It is the compressed, 1D summary of the $X$ data that best predicts $Y$.)C. Calculate the $X$ Loadings ($p_k$)Now we need to figure out how much of our original $X$ matrix is explained by this new score vector $t_k$. We do a simple linear regression of $X$ onto $t$:$$p_k = \frac{X_{k-1}^T t_k}{t_k^T t_k}$$(Intuition: Loadings tell us the structural relationship between the latent component and the original features. We need this to subtract the explained information out of $X$.)D. Calculate the $Y$ Loading/Coefficient ($q_k$)We also do a simple linear regression of $Y$ onto our new score vector $t_k$:$$q_k = \frac{Y_{k-1}^T t_k}{t_k^T t_k}$$(Intuition: This is the actual regression step! It tells us how much our latent component $t_k$ predicts the target $Y$.)Step 3: Deflation (The Clean-Up)Because we extract components one by one, we need to ensure that Component 2 does not just repeat the information found in Component 1. We do this by "deflating" (subtracting) the information we just explained from both $X$ and $Y$.Deflate $X$: Subtract the variance in $X$ that was explained by $t_k$.$$X_k = X_{k-1} - t_k p_k^T$$Deflate $Y$: Subtract the variance in $Y$ that was predicted by $t_k$.$$Y_k = Y_{k-1} - t_k q_k$$The new matrices $X_k$ and $Y_k$ now contain only the residual data—the noise and patterns that Component 1 failed to capture.Step 4: RepeatYou take your deflated matrices ($X_k$ and $Y_k$) and loop back to Step 2 to extract the next component. You repeat this $A$ times, where $A$ is the total number of latent components you want to keep (chosen via cross-validation).The Final Regression EquationOnce you have looped through and extracted all $A$ components, you have a set of $X$-weights ($W$), $X$-loadings ($P$), and $Y$-coefficients ($q$).Just like in PCR, you can mathematically transform these intermediate matrices back into a single vector of regression coefficients ($\hat{\beta}^{PLS}$) that maps directly to your original features:$$\hat{\beta}^{PLS} = W (P^T W)^{-1} q$$Your final prediction model is simply:$$\hat{Y} = X \hat{\beta}^{PLS}$$Why the Nitty-Gritty Makes PLS SuperiorIf you look closely at Step 2A ($w_k = X_{k-1}^T Y_{k-1}$), you see the magic of PLS.In PCR, the algorithm just looks for the loudest signal in $X$, regardless of whether it means anything. In PLS, the weight vector $w$ is generated by the dot product of $X$ and $Y$. If a feature in $X$ has a massive variance but zero correlation with $Y$, their dot product will be zero. PLS immediately suppresses that feature, effectively ignoring high-variance noise (like jaw-clenching in an EEG dataset) and preserving low-variance signals that genuinely drive the target variable (like the visual processing of an image).

# Multiplication Order

To understand the precise mechanics of why we use $V$, right-multiply to transform the data, and left-multiply to recover the coefficients, we have to look at the linear algebra underlying the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) and how it maps to the covariance matrix.Here is the mathematical breakdown of each of your questions.1. Why do we use $V$ and not $U$? (Feature Space vs. Sample Space)When you perform SVD on your mean-centered $n \times p$ data matrix $X$, you get:$$X = U D V^T$$$U$ is an $n \times n$ orthogonal matrix. Its columns span the sample (row) space.$D$ is an $n \times p$ diagonal matrix containing the singular values.$V$ is a $p \times p$ orthogonal matrix. Its columns span the feature (column) space.In Principal Component Regression (PCR), we care about finding the relationships between our features (e.g., how electrode 1 correlates with electrode 2). To find this, we look at the covariance matrix of $X$, which is proportional to $X^T X$.If we plug the SVD of $X$ into the covariance formula, we get:$$X^T X = (U D V^T)^T (U D V^T)$$$$X^T X = V D^T U^T U D V^T$$Because $U$ is an orthogonal matrix, $U^T U = I$ (the identity matrix). This collapses the equation to:$$X^T X = V D^2 V^T$$This is the exact eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix!The columns of $V$ are the eigenvectors (the Principal Components / Loadings).The diagonal elements of $D^2$ are the eigenvalues (the variance explained by each component).Conclusion: We use $V$ because its columns tell us exactly how our original features combine to create the directions of maximum variance. $U$ tells us how our samples relate to each other, which is not what we need to compute feature weights. (Note: The transformed scores $Z$ are mathematically equal to $U D$, so $U$ is implicitly used to represent the data in the new space, but $V$ is the mathematical bridge back to our features).2. Why do we use Right Multiplication ($X V$) to transform the data?When we want to transform our original data $X$ into the new, orthogonal Principal Component space $Z$, we are performing a change of basis. We want to project our $n$ samples onto the new feature axes defined by $V$.This requires right multiplication:$$Z = X V$$The Dimensionality Reason:$X$ is $n \times p$ (n samples, p original features).$V$ is $p \times p$ (p original features, p new principal components).By standard matrix multiplication rules, $(n \times p) \times (p \times p)$ results in an $n \times p$ matrix.The resulting matrix $Z$ has $n$ samples and $p$ new orthogonal features (scores).The Linear Algebra Reason:When you compute $Z = X V$, the first column of $Z$ (the first principal component score) is calculated by taking each row of $X$ and computing the dot product with the first column of $V$. It literally takes the row of raw feature values, weights them by the component loadings in $V$, and sums them up into a single new score. You can only apply column weights to rows via right multiplication.3. Why do we use Left Multiplication ($V_k \hat{\gamma}$) to transform back?In the PCR regression step, we regress $Y$ on the truncated score matrix $Z_k$ to get our latent coefficients $\hat{\gamma}$:$$\hat{Y} = Z_k \hat{\gamma}$$However, $\hat{\gamma}$ tells us the importance of the Principal Components, not our original features. To find out the importance of the original features ($\hat{\beta}^{PCR}$), we substitute $Z_k$ back into the equation.Since we know that $Z_k = X V_k$, we substitute it directly into the prediction equation:$$\hat{Y} = (X V_k) \hat{\gamma}$$Because matrix multiplication is associative, we can regroup the terms without changing the math:$$\hat{Y} = X (V_k \hat{\gamma})$$This equation is now in the exact format of a standard linear regression: $\hat{Y} = X \hat{\beta}$. Therefore, the term in the parentheses must be our original feature coefficients:$$\hat{\beta}^{PCR} = V_k \hat{\gamma}$$The Dimensionality Reason:$V_k$ is $p \times k$ (p original features, k principal components).$\hat{\gamma}$ is $k \times 1$ (k principal components, 1 target).By matrix multiplication rules, $(p \times k) \times (k \times 1)$ results in a $p \times 1$ vector.This output is exactly what we need: a single column vector of $p$ coefficients, providing one regression weight for every single original feature.
